# Module 3 — Value Chain & Pricing Power

_Ledger references: VC-01 to VC-07, RISK-01. Sources: S002, S008, S009, S010._

## Competitive structure

Grab is the regional share leader in both of its on-demand segments, but with meaningfully different competitive intensity by segment and by country:

- **Food delivery**: Grab ~54-55% of Southeast Asian GMV; Foodpanda a distant #2 regionally (~11%) but a closer #2 in Singapore (24%) and Malaysia (22%); ShopeeFood ~9-12%; Gojek(GoTo) ~10% (VC-01). Deliveroo's March 2026 exit from Singapore (~7% share vacated) is a modest near-term tailwind (VC-03).
- **Ride-hailing**: Grab dominant in Malaysia (>95%) and an estimated ~70% of combined SEA Mobility GMV, but GoTo/Gojek retains an estimated majority (~60%) of Indonesian ride-hailing — Grab's largest population market and a persistent competitive gap (VC-02).

Concentration is therefore **high at the regional level but genuinely contested at the country level** in Grab's single largest market (Indonesia), which matters because Indonesia is also the country most exposed to the Grab-GoTo merger speculation (C-01) — a combination there would resolve the one market where Grab does not already hold a commanding position.

## Barriers to entry/exit and switching costs

- **Network effects**: two-sided marketplace density (driver supply attracts riders/orders; order volume attracts drivers/merchants) is Grab's core moat, reinforced by superapp bundling (a Mobility user is a warm lead for Deliveries, GrabPay, and lending products).
- **Switching costs**: moderate — loyalty points, saved payment methods, and habitual app use create real but not insurmountable friction; Southeast Asian consumers are documented multi-homers (using Grab and Foodpanda/GoTo simultaneously), which caps Grab's pricing power relative to a true single-app-lock-in business.
- **Regulatory barriers**: mixed — ride-hailing licensing requirements provide some protection against new entrants in Mobility in several markets, while food delivery has comparatively low regulatory barriers to entry (a partial explanation for why Deliveries margins are structurally thinner than Mobility's).
- **Capital barriers**: Financial Services (digital banking) carries the highest capital/regulatory barrier to entry of the three segments (banking licenses, capital adequacy requirements), which is precisely why Grab is willing to fund years of losses there — the option value of an incumbent license position.

## Supplier concentration and terms

Grab's key "suppliers" are driver-partners and merchant-partners, both highly fragmented (no single driver or restaurant chain has pricing leverage over Grab) but collectively powerful through **regulatory and labor-organizing channels** rather than direct commercial negotiation. This is the actual locus of the most acute near-term pricing-power test:

- **VC-05 / RISK-01**: Vietnam's National Competition Commission opened a review of Grab's fare-setting, commission and fee/deduction policies in September 2026, triggered by a driver-led weekend work-stoppage call over commission levels. This is a live test of whether Grab can sustain current take rates without regulatory intervention in one of its larger markets.
- Grab classifies driver-partners as independent contractors across ASEAN; a reclassification ruling in any major market would raise costs and could force commission/pricing changes, with limited ability to pass costs through to consumers given competitive intensity (RISK-01).

## Customer concentration and pricing mechanisms

Consumers are atomized (no concentration risk), but **procurement power sits with the platform, not the customer** in the near term — Grab sets fares/prices algorithmically, subject to competitive response. The Vietnam regulatory review (above) is precisely a test of whether that price-setting power itself becomes constrained by policy rather than by competition.

## Pricing-power stress test

**VC-04**: FY2025 provides one genuine, if partial, positive data point: Grab expanded both Deliveries (2.0% vs 1.65% implied FY2024) and Mobility (8.7% vs 8.6% FY2024) segment Adjusted EBITDA margins *simultaneously* with 19-21% GMV growth — margin expansion did not require volume or share sacrifice. This is real evidence of pricing power (or at least monetization power, via advertising) rather than assumption. However:

- This test has **not** yet been run against a well-funded, motivated competitive response. Maybank's scenario work (S010) estimates that "moderate" competitive intensity could still cut 2030 Deliveries EBITDA by ~23% versus base case, and "aggressive" competition (e.g., a ShopeeFood/Meituan-backed Foodpanda) could cut it by ~41% — i.e., the pricing-power conclusion is **provisional**, not proven, and Deliveries (not Mobility) is where it is most exposed.
- **VC-06**: where margin compression would land if a price war resumed: primarily on **Grab and Foodpanda's own P&Ls** via partner/consumer incentives, not on restaurant merchants or consumers, based on FY2025 behavior (partner incentives +33% YoY, consumer incentives +17% YoY, both growing alongside — not faster than — GMV even in a relatively benign competitive year). Incentive intensity, not headline commission rates, is the variable to watch.

## Historical stress period

Grab's own FY2022-FY2023 history (Adjusted EBITDA of -$793m and -$22m respectively, EE ledger/RG-01) is itself the clearest historical stress period: a multi-year, capital-intensive competitive battle (principally against Gojek pre-GoTo-merger and against multiple food-delivery entrants) that only resolved into structural profitability from FY2024 onward. This is direct evidence that Grab **can and has** absorbed years of margin compression to defend share when competitively necessary — a fact that cuts both ways: it validates Grab's willingness and balance-sheet capacity to fight (relevant if Foodpanda re-escalates), but it also confirms that the current profitability is not an immutable structural floor.

## Durable moat vs. cyclical advantage

**Mixed, segment-dependent.** Mobility's regional scale, licensing position, and higher switching costs support a genuinely durable moat. Deliveries' current margin gains look more like a **cyclical advantage** (a temporarily benign competitive window plus an advertising monetization wave) layered on top of a durable but thinner structural position (superapp distribution, logistics density) — consistent with the Module 4 (Cycle vs. Structure) conclusion.
