# Evidence and synthesis

## Source discipline

Use the most direct credible source available for each claim. A practical order is:

1. company filings, earnings materials, investor presentations, and exchange disclosures for reported company data;
2. regulators, statistical agencies, industry bodies, and official benchmark providers for market size, volumes, prices, and rules;
3. reputable primary research or clearly attributed channel checks for estimates and industry structure;
4. financial databases and market-data services for standardized prices, shares, and consensus, with the date and methodology recorded;
5. reputable secondary reporting for context or triangulation, not as a substitute for primary evidence when the claim is material.

Capture source ID, title, publisher, URL or document identifier, publication date, observation period, access date, and the exact claim or data supported. Do not cite a landing page when a filing, table, or dated release is available. If sources conflict, preserve both, explain the definition or timing difference, and identify which one drives the analysis.

## Fact, estimate, inference

Use explicit labels:

- **Observation:** directly reported or measured data.
- **Calculation:** arithmetic derived from stated observations.
- **Estimate:** a source's forecast or the researcher's modeled value.
- **Assumption:** an input chosen for a scenario or normalization.
- **Inference:** a conclusion drawn from one or more records.
- **Research gap:** a material unanswered question or unavailable series.

Do not turn an estimate into a fact through prose. Put confidence and limitations near the claim, especially for market-size studies, private-company share estimates, TAM figures, and forward consensus.

## Reader-facing structure

Unless the user requests another format, use this sequence:

1. **Executive summary** — sector definition, current state, key drivers, debate, and what to monitor.
2. **Scope and taxonomy** — included businesses, exclusions, geography, dates, and definitions.
3. **How the industry works** — value chain, customers, revenue pools, cost and capital structure, KPIs.
4. **Structural and cyclical dynamics** — competition, regulation, technology, supply/demand, and macro transmission.
5. **Sector dashboard** — historical and forward data, methodology, and interpretation.
6. **Comparable companies** — peer rationale, operating comparison, valuation, and important non-comparables.
7. **Investor debate and scenarios** — competing views, sensitivities, catalysts, risks, and falsification signals.
8. **Monitoring framework** — leading indicators, reporting cadence, and triggers for an update.
9. **Sources and gaps** — source registry, definitions, unresolved conflicts, and data limitations.

Explain jargon on first use and put units in table headers. Use short interpretation paragraphs after quantitative tables. A primer should leave the reader able to predict which data will matter next, not merely able to repeat the current numbers.

## Claims and citations

Place citations next to the material claim or table. Use a source note for calculated tables listing the input sources, period, formula, and aggregation method. Distinguish the market's current valuation from the researcher's view of fair value; avoid implying a target price or investment recommendation unless the user explicitly asks for one.

## Final QA questions

- Could a reader reproduce the material numbers from the source note?
- Is every comparison economically and temporally like-for-like?
- Does each major section explain a mechanism, not just a fact?
- Are uncertainty, outliers, conflicts, and missing data visible?
- Does the conclusion follow from the scope and evidence rather than from the most available company data?
